When I began reading this book, I didn't know much about the history of religion. I'm most knowledgeable about Christianity, but the topics of Judaism and Islam are almost unfamiliar to me. In this enormous book, the author attempts to present the evolution of the concept of God in these three major world religions.

It wasn't an easy or quick read. The chapters are long, and Armstrong cites numerous historical figures and events, which is certainly not surprising in a book of this type. The sheer number overwhelmed me, and I found it difficult to navigate through the pages as I read about the various philosophers and religious movements of the early millennium.

The author's writing was also somewhat repetitive—using the same phrases, words, and themes. Overall, I consider this a positive, as the book is a great source of vast knowledge and starting points for further exploration of religious studies.

For someone like me, for whom religion has never played any role in her life, the author's theses make sense. The ways in which ancient peoples transformed their pantheons of gods and adapted their beliefs to changing circumstances seem logical to me and align with my historical knowledge. However, for a believer, this book may be problematic. This is primarily due to its straightforward description of these processes and its stripping of any sacredness, which is likely essential for followers of Christianity, Islam, and Judaism. It is not without reason that this book is considered controversial and bears the hallmarks of a critique of organized religion, particularly Christianity. I felt that the passages about Islam and other faiths (Buddhism and Hinduism) were met with greater understanding and milder criticism from Armstrong. However, as I mentioned earlier, as a non-believer, the author's preferences in this regard pose no problem.
