I have recently come across references to Ayn ​​Rand several times in various articles and podcasts. Wishing to familiarize myself with her work, I began with one of her earliest and shortest novellas.

Set in an unspecified future, society looks entirely different from the one we know today. Technological progress is carefully planned and kept hidden from the majority of people. Individual needs and desires no longer matter; the highest value is labor for the collective—for society as a whole. It is even forbidden to refer to oneself as "I." Only the crowd and the collective hold any significance. An all-knowing Council monitors every move of the individuals and guards the established order.
People have no names, only codes or symbols. The protagonist and narrator of *Anthem* is called Equality-2521, and he presents the world from the perspective of a street sweeper. Upon accidentally gaining access to knowledge from the past, he begins conducting experiments, eventually discovering electricity. In doing so, he becomes an individual unwanted by the Council; he is too independent, stands out from the rest, and poses a potential threat to the prevailing order.

The novella serves as a manifesto for the author’s views—ideas that are said to be expressed even more strongly in her subsequent novels (*Atlas Shrugged* and *The Fountainhead*), which I intend to read soon. The author critiques the concept of collectivism and the idea of ​​working for the "common good." One can see here the seeds of the philosophical movement she founded: objectivism. This becomes understandable once one learns about Ayn Rand’s own life story. As a young person, she witnessed the Bolshevik Revolution, and her family was among its victims.
This short text is, of course, insufficient to present the entire philosophical system that the author advocates. From today’s perspective, the work might seem naive and overly simplistic. However, the novella is brief enough that anyone can read it and decide for themselves whether they wish to delve deeper into the controversial school of thought known as objectivism.
