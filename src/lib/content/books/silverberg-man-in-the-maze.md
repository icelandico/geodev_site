This book was my first encounter with the work of Robert Silverberg many years ago. He is one of the authors who sparked my fascination with the science fiction genre—a genre that holds a particularly significant place in my literary tastes.

In the distant future, humanity is colonizing ever-greater expanses of space. This creates a need for diplomatic missions to engage with newly discovered species and races inhabiting the universe. One such diplomat is the protagonist, Richard Mueller. However, following a visit to a newly discovered planet, he undergoes a catastrophic transformation. In the aftermath, he chooses self-imposed exile from humanity, settling on the distant planet of Lemnos—specifically, within the labyrinth that gives the book its title. Eventually, however, the need arises for Richard to return to Earth—a return that must take place against his will.

I appreciate the novel’s narrative style. The protagonist’s story and his motivations are not presented outright; instead, they unfold across several chapters, with new layers of his past revealed as the plot progresses.

The book’s climax is a confrontation between the protagonist and his former friend, Boardman. This encounter is primarily verbal—it is the dialogue and the exchange of ideas between these two characters that represent the core value the author intended to convey to the reader.
This is the type of science fiction I value most. It is not driven by an abundance of action or "adventure." Rather, the novel consists largely of dialogues and flashbacks that illuminate the characters' decision-making processes, offering insight into their worldviews, weaknesses, and inner reflections. The fantastical backdrop—distant worlds, alien races, and advanced technologies—serves to enrich the narrative.

I did find one jarring aspect in this novel: the way women are portrayed. They appear merely as "decoration" for the men—functioning as a kind of trophy or an object for the satisfaction of male needs.
This is something I’ve noticed in other works by Robert Silverberg; I take it as a sign of the era in which they were written.

Robert Silverberg is an author worth reading. "The Man in the Maze" is a successful blend of science fiction and philosophical elements that never become overly pretentious.
