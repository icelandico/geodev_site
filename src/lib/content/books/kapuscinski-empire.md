The titular "Empire" concerns the Russian Empire and its successor, first the communist Soviet Union and later the Russian Federation. In this reportage, the author describes his experiences from numerous trips to Russia and the Soviet republics over several decades.

There is no central theme to this reportage. These are the author's free-form yet structured observations and experiences, focusing on the people he meets and their stories. The foundation is the history of the nations that comprised the Empire – Armenians, Georgians, Turkmens, Russians and others. As a backdrop for the narrative, the author frequently cites events relevant to the people and places he meets. The book is not long, so the historical outlines are also very general, but they serve as a good complement to the accounts of the people described. Well-known and "high-profile" events are presented here, such as the famine in Ukraine in the 1930s, the problems of Nagorno-Karabakh, and the ecological tragedy of the Aral Sea. The reportage also includes many local and seemingly small stories, such as the artistic work of a painter in Tbilisi or the fate of miners in Norilsk.

Kapuściński's reflections on the functioning of this vast but flawed Empire are crucial. The distrust of ordinary citizens toward outsiders (including himself), the militarization of social life, and the general misery and poverty are masked by a growing awareness of the Empire's grandeur, its vastness, limitless resources, and aggressive historical policy.

Many of these reflections remain relevant. Russia remains distrustful, aggressive, and eager to repress both its own population and its former republics, which chose to pursue a path of development without Russia. Despite the changes in its borders and political system, the mentality of the "Empire" state apparatus has unfortunately remained unchanged.
